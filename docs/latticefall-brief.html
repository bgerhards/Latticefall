00Premise

A dead civilization left the doors open. Something has been walking through them for a long time.

You command Task Force Meridian, holding transit anchors on the far side of a precursor network humanity does not understand and cannot switch off. You do not have enough power. You never will.

01Core hook

Power is the real currency

Every tower defense game meters you with money. Money is a decision you make once, at build time, and then forget.

Latticefall meters you with continuous power draw. Cash buys the emplacement; the reactor bus pays for it every second it stays online. Capacity is fixed per anchor and rises only through story beats — never through grinding.

The consequence is that a built board is not a solved board. When a heavy wave forces the shield wall online, something else has to go dark, and you choose which while the wave is already on the field. Overdraw doesn't fail you outright — it browns out the whole bus, so the punishment for greed is a board that still fights, just badly. That keeps failure legible instead of sudden.

Draw, not cost

Emplacements have a build price and a standing megawatt draw. The second number is the one that hurts.

Live triage

Systems toggle mid-wave with no refund and a short spin-up. Cutting the scan relay to afford one more turret volley is a real play.

Brownout, not bankruptcy

Exceeding capacity degrades every system by 40% rather than blocking the build. Recoverable, readable, and it punishes without ambushing.

Testable by construction

Power is a scalar over time, so an entire anchor can be simulated headless and graded without rendering a frame.

02The board

True 2:1 isometric

Tiles are diamonds at a 2:1 ratio, which fixes the camera at 26.5651° elevation — atan(1/2). Every asset is authored in 3D and rendered orthographically at that exact angle, at four yaw rotations, so the entire game shares one lighting rig and one projection. Consistency is enforced by the pipeline rather than by discipline.

03Campaign

Twenty-four anchors

Three acts of eight. Each act introduces one antagonist, one biome, one power-capacity tier, and one mechanic that invalidates the previous act's dominant strategy. Difficulty is authored as data, not code, so the curve below is a target the headless simulator grades every build against.

Act I
01 — 08

Dead Air

Meridian steps through to an anchor that has been transmitting a carrier signal for eleven years with nobody on the other end. The opposition is automated: Ordinal wardens still executing a containment order whose subject nobody has identified. Teaches power, placement, and the fact that the network's builders were afraid of something.

Act II
09 — 16

Salvage Rights

Someone got here first. A private recovery outfit has been stripping anchors for eighteen months under a contract no government will admit to signing, and they are better funded than you. Human opposition means adaptive tactics: they cut your bus, they target the reactor, they learn your build order.

Act III
17 — 24

The Hollow

The containment order had a subject. It has been using the network as a circulatory system for a very long time, and every anchor Meridian has powered up was a door held open for it. The wardens were never the enemy. Act III is a fighting retreat toward the only anchor that can still be closed from this side.

04Voice

Grounded, dry, under pressure

Dialog runs as portrait-and-typewriter exchanges: a short brief before each anchor, two or three interruptions during waves, one debrief. No voice acting — the budget goes to writing volume and portrait animation instead. Mid-wave lines are interruptible and never block input.

The register is professionals doing dangerous technical work, not heroes making speeches. Exposition arrives as shop talk, and the horror lands because the people describing it are bored.

Nomenclature → every proper noun is original. No terminology carried over from the influencing series — the network is the Lattice, its nodes are anchors, its builders the Ordinal. A nomenclature bible gets written before any dialog, because renaming forty assets and three hundred lines afterward is the expensive kind of mistake.
