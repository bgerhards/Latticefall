The test of the instrument was red, and no tier ran it

BALPLC

The entry before this one proved the coverage instrument's geometric half does not predict which emplacements actually work. This is the repair: presence-weighted coverage becomes the predictor, lane_coverage() stays as a measurement because it is still a true statement about a level, and rank correlation against measured uptime goes from +0.520 to +0.748 across the whole game — +0.622 to +0.780 on the eighteen anchors last week's pass never touched, which is the number that is not circular. Underneath it was something worse: sim/coverage.py --selftest, the only runtime proof that the instrumented sim cannot change an outcome, had been failing on main for at least two merges, because no gate tier runs it.

The previous entry, an hour old as this is written, ended on a diagnosis: sim/coverage.py's geometric lane_coverage() does not predict its own dynamic tail, and the slot with 17% geometric coverage was doing three times the work of the slot with 44%. That entry re-sited 21 slots on the strength of a scratch implementation of presence-weighted coverage. This one makes the replacement real, quantifies how much better it is, and finds that the test which guards the whole instrument had been red the entire time.

lane_coverage() is kept, not deleted. It answers a true question about a level — what fraction of the lane's length falls inside a slot's range — and that question deserves an honest answer. What changed is that it is no longer the thing the summary and the verdict label are computed from.

The 18-anchor row is the one that matters. The six re-sited anchors were optimised against presence last week, so a gain measured on them could be circular — the metric scoring well on the boards it authored. It holds on the eighteen that were never touched, and that is what makes it a real result rather than a tautology.

Within-anchor ordering — which is the question PLC-04's candidate lattice actually asks, since it never compares a slot on one board against a slot on another — went from mean rho +0.442 to +0.773. And lane_coverage() is outright negative on three anchors: anchor-02 at −0.301, anchor-10 at −0.244, anchor-13 at −0.019. On those boards it orders their own slots backwards. Presence-weighted coverage is positive on all 24.

The four inversion rows, recomputed

Two of the four slots the previous entry used to make its case — anchor-13 (6,0) and anchor-10 (7,13) — no longer exist. 5021a9e deleted them, which was the point of that pass. They were restored into an in-memory copy of each anchor so that all four could be measured identically under today's code. That detail is what makes these numbers reproducible a year from now, and it is why the uptime figures differ slightly from the ones published an hour ago.

lane_coverage() ranks those four 36.8 > 43.9 > 36.9 > 17.1, against a true order of 54.0 > 23.5 > 0.6 > 0.4. It is inverted at both ends — it calls the best slot the worst and the worst slot the second best. Presence gets the order right.

One more, straight out of the shipped --detail output: anchor-10 (11,9) has the highest lane coverage on the whole anchor at 71.7%, and 29.2% presence. The single most attractive position on that board by the old instrument is a third-rate one by the new.

verdict() was calling the best-sited anchor in the game badly sited

The label verdict() printed for anchor-01 — 83% median uptime, a 5% near-zero tail, the healthiest board there is — was this:

(a) geometry: even 2x range does not recover coverage — slots are far from the lane.verdict(), on anchor-01, before this pass

The new labels compare an anchor's authored slots against its own board's ceiling: the median of the N best legal positions, where N is that anchor's own slot count, and legality comes from the engine's own _placement_reason() rather than from a re-derivation of the rule. Median-of-N against median-of-N, so no absolute presence threshold is needed — which matters, because presence share is not calibrated to uptime and a fixed cut on it would not be defensible.

Four outcomes, and they land where the evidence already was:

  • no material near-zero tail — 12 anchors, including anchor-01, which needed no diagnosis at all.
  • (b) range/waves — anchors 02–05, the four lowest board ceilings in the game. LF-187 independently says decision 074's range numbers must be re-derived; this arrives at the same four boards from a different direction.
  • (a) siting — anchors 06, 07, 09 and 11. Slots that a better position on the same board would fix.
  • (c) not geometry — anchors 08, 10 and 13, which is exactly LF-218's finding: re-siting moves guns into the live zone but cannot lengthen the live zone.

The worse thing underneath

sim/coverage.py --selftest is the only runtime proof that InstrumentedSim cannot change an Outcome. It runs the same (anchor, policy, difficulty) through both Sim and InstrumentedSim and asserts the results are field-for-field identical. Without it, a rules change in an unrelated workstream can silently invalidate every coverage measurement this project takes.

It was failing on main, and had been for at least two merges, because no gate tier runs it. Nothing was red anywhere. Nothing could be.

The mechanism is almost funny. The selftest builds a probe at (500,500) to prove that a slot with zero coverage measures zero. PLC-02 gave the engine a real legality predicate, which started answering out_of_bounds for that position — so the scheduled build silently stopped landing, and the check failed with “the scheduled build never landed”: a message about the harness, not about coverage. A metric assertion that had quietly become a harness assertion.

Proved rather than assumed, by running git show HEAD:sim/coverage.py in isolation, which printed HEAD placed slots: []. Fixed by widening the in-memory probe anchor to 40×40 and moving the probe to (30,30) — still exactly zero coverage at the longest range in the game — and no file under data/ was touched. Filed as LF-221, where the bug is fixed and the gap is the item: the selftest is about 2m43s in full, tier 2 is already over its own budget (LF-178), so it needs either tier 3/4 or a cheap single-anchor outcome-parity variant at tier 2.

The new assertions were proved red first

House style here, and it was actually done rather than claimed — by injecting bugs into an in-memory copy and confirming each new assertion fires:

The refusal that was licensed and did not apply

The brief explicitly offered an out: if the presence profile turned out to be policy-dependent enough that a single pooled profile per anchor was meaningless, say so and do not ship. That out was measured rather than taken. Each of the 60 (policy, difficulty) cells was scored against its own profile and against the pooled one, mean within-cell rank correlation over 6 anchors:

profile usedmean within-cell rho
lane_coverage()+0.487
pooled per-anchor profile (what shipped)+0.828
own-cell oracle (not shippable)+0.865

Pooling costs 0.037 of a 0.378 gain — it captures about 96% of what a per-cell oracle would buy. The refusal was licensed and did not apply, which is only worth knowing because it was checked instead of assumed.

But the per-run spread is real and wide: the worst slot swings 83.6 percentage points across runs on anchor-02. So it is reported rather than averaged away — per_run_min, per_run_max and per_run_std on every record, and a per-run lo..hi column in --detail. A mean that hides an 83-point spread is the same class of error as the metric this entry replaces.

Cost, and the gate

+6.3% to +9.8% wall clock; a full 24-anchor run is 2m11s at --jobs 8. There is no new simulation — the presence profile is accumulated by the same walk that already computes uptime. One file changed: sim/coverage.py, 589 → 1008 lines. No engine change, no data change, no game value moved.

The gate ran at tier 4: 38 passed, 0 failed, 2 skipped. The two skips are rules parity and rules parity (windows), skipped by the content-hash cache because digest f3e5a7b01020 had been verified fresh 40 minutes earlier against an identical set of rule and data files. That is cached, not re-run, and a skip is not a pass — said that way on purpose, in an entry that is entirely about instruments reporting something other than what they measure. sim determinism passed on this run.

Caveats that belong on the record

  • The letter is a pointer to a column, not a result. The classification is coarse at the boundary — anchor-11 at 0.72 and anchor-08 at 0.78 read as different letters two points apart. The docstring says so, so a reader of the tool learns it at the same time as a reader of this entry.
  • The label is now relative to two things, not one: range_basis.digest (LF-186) and the slot layout, because presence is measured on the authored board. lane_coverage() only ever needed the first caveat. A verdict computed before a re-siting pass is not comparable with one computed after — including, specifically, the verdicts in the entry before this one.
  • Presence share is still not calibrated to uptime. It predicts ordering well and says nothing reliable about magnitude, which is why every threshold in the new verdict() is relative.

Three times now

Two entries in a row where the fault was in the instrument rather than in the code. The previous one found the metric measuring the wrong quantity; this one found that the test of that metric had been failing unnoticed, because nothing ran it.

That is the same move as PLC-07's the fault was in the instrument, not the code, and the same move as LF-201's it reported a destruction it was incapable of performing. The habit is not scepticism about the code. It is scepticism about the thing doing the measuring, and it has now paid three times on unrelated work.

What this leaves for PLC-04, the next workstream: a public API of presence_coverage(), bucket_points(), legal_positions() and board_presence_ceiling(). The last of those is already the argmax over the candidate lattice PLC-04 needs, so that work starts with its search function written and measured.

The work sits on lf/bal-presence-coverage and was not yet committed when this entry was written, so no commit hash is listed — the same call as last time, and for the same reason: a hash written before the commit exists is a claim this journal has no business making.
